Where do the world’s extreme poor live?

If we look at the number of people in extreme poverty by continent, extreme poverty has shifted from Asia to Africa, with East Asia and the Pacific seeing the largest gains in the last 15 years. Regional shares of world extreme poverty moved from 30% to 69% for Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), from 30% to 7% for South Asia, and from 28% to 6% for East Asia and the Pacific from 2010 to 2024 (Figure 1a). If we look at the shift in the shares of extreme poverty by income class in the same period, the share of middle-income countries (MICs) moved from 74% to 55%, and the share of low-income countries (LICs) moved from 26% to 44% (Figure 1b). The share of extreme poverty in MICs remains high, with five SSA countries - Nigeria, Tanzania, Kenya, Angola, and Ghana accounting for 42% of the extreme poverty count in middle-income countries.

Is aid flowing to the poorest countries?

Figure 2 plots the aid per person in extreme poverty for middle- and low-income countries by dividing the total Official Development Assistance (ODA) received by countries classified as LICs or MICs in that year by the number of people in extreme poverty in those countries. From 2020 to 2024, for MICs this figure has gone up from $168 to $266, while for LICs it has gone down from $176 to $119, making the MICs/LICs ratio move from 0.95x to 2.23x. Since Ukraine accounts for a large share of the post-2021 increase in aid going to MICs, excluding it moves the MICs/LICs ratio from 0.93x to 1.55x.

Figure 3 plots a measure of the progressivity of ODA from 1990 to 2024, which calculates the share of aid going to the poorest recipient countries accounting for 20% of the total population. This figure has decreased from 62% at its peak in 2018 to 46% in 2024. From both Figures 2 and 3, the trends in aid going to the poorest countries are declining. The World Bank’s IDA arm that lends to the poorest countries shows a similar picture. Mathiasen, Landers, and Martinez (2026) find that across FY20-25, 42% of IDA financing volumes go to gap and blend countries on average, leaving only 54% for IDA-only countries. In FY25, countries above the operational income threshold ($1,335 for FY25) received 38% of the grant element in IDA (Figure 4).

Figure 5 shows ODA per capita for all recipient countries and outlines them in red if they experienced more than 1000 deaths from state-based armed conflict in that year. In SSA, ODA per capita does not have a consistent relationship with both conflict and extreme poverty. Among the seven countries outlined in red in 2024, Nigeria receives $18 per head and Somalia $133. Nor does higher aid per head go to the poorest: Namibia, a MIC where 23% live in extreme poverty, receives $112 per head, against $39 in Madagascar, a LIC, where 68% do. The four middle-income countries with the most extremely poor people receive between $6 and $55 per head in 2024 (Angola $6, Nigeria $18, Tanzania $48, Kenya $55).

How do major donors perform on lending to the poorest countries?

Figure 6 plots the flows from major donors to recipients, classified by either income group or continent (a button switches between the two on the figure). Among the largest OECD country donors shown (the US, Canada, Germany, the UK, France and Japan), the largest share of each donor’s net ODA goes to regional or unallocated categories. (aid not assigned to a specific recipient country) This ranges from 40% for the US to 69% for the UK in 2024, except France and Japan, whose largest share goes to lower-MICs (41% and 56%). EU Institutions sent 62% of their net ODA in 2024 to UMICs and 60% to Europe, largely reflecting Ukraine, which alone received 54% of it. Multilateral organizations sent 47% of their net ODA in 2024 to LMICs and 29% to LICs. LICs received 30% of their ODA from multilateral organizations and another 21% from the US. Looking at recipients by continent, in 2024 Africa got 42% of its ODA from multilaterals and another 21% from the US; other donors and donor groups each provided no more than 8%. 21 of the 25 low-income recipient countries are in Africa, and 63% of Africa’s ODA comes from just two sources: multilateral organisations and the United States.
