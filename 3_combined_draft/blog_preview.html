Is aid still reaching the world’s poorest countries?

By Rachel Glennerster and Aishwarya Venkat

[Intro to add]

Where do those in extreme poverty live?

From 2010 to 2024, the concentration of extreme poverty1 has shifted to Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Regional shares of world extreme poverty shifted from 30% to 69% for SSA, and from about 30% to under 10% for South and East Asia (Figure 1a). The concentration also increased in low-income countries (LICs)2 but about half of it remains in lower-middle-income countries (LMICs). The share of LICs in lower-and middle-income countries (L&MICs) rose from 26% to 45%, while the share of LMICs barely moved from 51% to 49% (Figure 1b). Most of that half in LMICs is in SSA: in 2024, the region held 19% of the LMIC population but accounted for 59% of the extreme poor in LMICs. Nigeria alone has 24% of the extreme poor in LMICs and holds 7% of the LMIC population.

Is aid flowing to the poorest countries?

LICs’ Official Development Assistance (ODA) shares have not kept up with their increased share of extreme poverty. Zooming in on recent years, in 2020, LICs held 40% of the extreme poor in low- and middle-income countries (L&MICs) and received 41% of their net ODA3. By 2024, those figures had moved to 45% and 27%, respectively. Excluding Ukraine from middle-income countries (MICs) due to the war-related aid spike in the region, LICs still received only 34% of net ODA in 2024. Upper-middle-income countries (UMICs), even after excluding Ukraine, received 15% of net ODA and held 6% of the extreme poor (Figure 2).

A second measure shows that the share of aid flowing to the poorest countries has decreased. For 1990-2024, Figure 3 plots the share of aid going to the poorest recipient countries holding 20% of the total world population.4 This share has decreased from 62% at its peak in 2018 to 46% in 2024.

Even the World Bank’s International Development Association (IDA) arm, which lends to the poorest countries, sends more than a third of its concessional funding to countries above its own income threshold. Forthcoming work by Mathiasen, Landers, and Martinez shows that in FY25, countries above IDA’s operational income threshold5 ($1,335 for FY25) received 38% of the grant element in IDA. The same authors' recent paper finds that across FY20-25, 42% of IDA financing volumes go to gap and blend countries on average, leaving only 54% for IDA-only countries. By World Bank income class, on average in FY20-25 LICs got 44% of IDA allocations and 62% of the grant element (Figure 4).

In SSA, ODA per capita does not have a consistent relationship with either conflict or extreme poverty. Figure 5 shows ODA per capita for all recipient countries and outlines them in red if they experienced more than 1000 deaths from state-based armed conflict in that year. Ghana, an LMIC with about 33% extreme poverty, got $81 ODA per head in 2024. Uganda and Madagascar, both LICs, got $44 and $39 of ODA per head and had 56% and 68% extreme poverty, respectively. Ghana, Uganda, and Madagascar have populations between 30 and 50 million and didn’t record severe armed conflict in the last five years. Among similarly sized LICs marked in conflict (19-25 million people), ODA per head in 2024 varied from $45 in Mali to $133 in Somalia.

How do major donors perform on ODA to the poorest countries?

The poorest countries depend on a few donors. In 2024, LICs got 30% of their ODA from multilaterals, and another 21% from the United States. Africa had 21 of the 25 LICs, and 63% of Africa’s ODA came from just two sources: multilateral organizations and the United States; no other donor contributed more than 8% of Africa's ODA (Figure 6). Among the largest OECD country donors shown (the US, Canada, Germany, the UK, France and Japan), the largest share of each donor’s net ODA goes to regional or unallocated categories (aid not assigned to a specific recipient country). This ranges from 40% for the US to 69% for the UK in 2024. France and Japan are exceptions; their largest share went to LMICs (41% and 56%). No major country donor sent more than 15% of its total net ODA to LICs. In 2024, EU Institutions sent 62% of their net ODA to UMICs (largely reflecting Ukraine, which alone received 54% of it), and only 8% to LICs.

Can low-income countries fill the gap themselves?

Ending extreme poverty in LICs would take a far larger share of their income compared to LMICs, and LICs have few alternative sources of inflows. Sahoo et al. 2025 estimate the cost of targeted transfers to bring countries’ extreme poverty rate to 1% as a share of GDP. Targeting challenges notwithstanding, after grouping their results by World Bank income classification (Figure 7), we can see that for 13 out of 17 LICs, this cost is more than 10% of GDP compared to 2 out of 13 LMICs. For Nigeria, which has one of the highest numbers of extreme poor in the sample, the cost is 6%. LICs also have smaller non-ODA foreign inflows. Figure 8 compares net ODA, personal remittances, and foreign direct investment (FDI) net inflows across LICs, LMICs, and UMICs. For LICs, per capita ODA averaged $76 in 2020-24 compared to $23 in remittances and $30 in FDI. For LMICs and UMICs, corresponding ODA was $21 and $13, remittances were $128 and $84, and FDI was $43 and $168.

Conclusion

While our OECD ODA data used in this blog stops in 2024, a related CGD blog projects how announced ODA cuts in 2025-2026 compare to 2023 ODA levels. The authors conclude that 17 L&MICs are projected to suffer a relative decrease in Gross National Disposable Income (GNDI) of more than 1%, 9 lose more than 2%, and 5 lose more than 3%. The blog projects a loss of 6.1% of 2023 GNI for Somalia – out of which 5.05 percentage points come from the US alone. Relying on a few donors can leave the poorest countries vulnerable to major cuts. Donors weighing further cuts should take into account the poorest countries’ dependence on them.
