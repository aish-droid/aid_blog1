By Rachel Glennerster and Aishwarya Venkat

Where do the world’s extreme poor live?

If we look at the number of people in extreme poverty by continent, extreme poverty has shifted from Asia to Africa, with East Asia and the Pacific seeing the largest gains in the last 15 years. Regional shares of world extreme poverty moved from 30% to 69% for Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), from 30% to 7% for South Asia, and from 28% to 6% for East Asia and the Pacific from 2010 to 2024 (Figure 1a). If we look at the shift in the shares of extreme poverty by income class in the same period, the share of middle-income countries (MICs)1 moved from 74% to 55%, and the share of low-income countries (LICs) moved from 26% to 44% (Figure 1b). The share of extreme poverty in MICs remains high, especially in Africa. Five SSA countries - Nigeria, Tanzania, Kenya, Angola, and Ghana account for 42% of the extreme poor in middle-income countries.2

Is aid flowing to the poorest countries?

In 2020, LICs held 41% of the world’s extreme poor and received 40% of all net ODA. By 2024, those figures had moved to 45% and 27%, respectively. Excluding Ukraine from MICs due to the post-conflict aid spike in the region, LICs still received only 34% of net ODA in 2024. UMICs, even after excluding Ukraine, receive 15% of net ODA while holding only 6% of extreme poor (See Figure 2).

Figure 3 plots a measure of the progressivity of ODA from 1990 to 2024, which calculates the share of aid going to the poorest recipient countries accounting for 20% of the total world population3. This figure has decreased from 62% at its peak in 2018 to 46% in 2024. Both Figures 2 and 3 show that the proportion of aid going to the poorest countries has declined.

The World Bank’s IDA arm that lends to the poorest countries shows a similar picture. Mathiasen, Landers, and Martinez (2026) find that across FY20-25, 42% of IDA financing volumes go to gap and blend countries on average, leaving only 54% for IDA-only countries. In FY25, countries above the operational income threshold4 ($1,335 for FY25) received 38% of the grant element in IDA. By income classes that we have used so far, on average in FY20-25 LICs got 44% of IDA allocations, and only 62% of the grant element. (Figure 4 shows the allocation and grant element by income classification and details IDA lending status).

Figure 5 shows ODA per capita for all recipient countries and outlines them in red if they experienced more than 1000 deaths from state-based armed conflict in that year. In SSA, ODA per capita does not have a consistent relationship with both conflict and extreme poverty. Among the seven countries outlined in red in 2024, Nigeria receives $18 per head and Somalia $133. Nor does higher aid per head go to the poorest: Namibia, a MIC where 23% live in extreme poverty, receives $112 per head, against $39 in Madagascar, a LIC, where 68% do. The four middle-income countries with the most extremely poor people receive between $6 and $55 per head in 2024 (Angola $6, Nigeria $18, Tanzania $48, Kenya $55).

How do major donors perform on lending to the poorest countries?

Figure 6 plots the flows from major donors to recipients, classified by either income group or continent (a button switches between the two on the figure). Among the largest OECD country donors shown (the US, Canada, Germany, the UK, France and Japan), the largest share of each donor’s net ODA goes to regional or unallocated categories. (aid not assigned to a specific recipient country) This ranges from 40% for the US to 69% for the UK in 2024, except France and Japan, whose largest share goes to lower-MICs (41% and 56%). EU Institutions sent 62% of their net ODA in 2024 to UMICs and 60% to Europe, largely reflecting Ukraine, which alone received 54% of it. Multilateral organizations sent 47% of their net ODA in 2024 to LMICs and 29% to LICs. LICs received 30% of their ODA from multilateral organizations and another 21% from the US. Looking at recipients by continent, in 2024 Africa got 42% of its ODA from multilaterals and another 21% from the US; other donors and donor groups each provided no more than 8%. 21 of the 25 low-income recipient countries are in Africa, and 63% of Africa’s ODA comes from just two sources: multilateral organizations and the United States.

Cost of ending extreme poverty: LICs need more support

Sahoo et al. 2025 estimate the cost of targeted transfers to bring countries’ extreme poverty rate to 1% as a share of GDP. Targeting challenges notwithstanding, after grouping their results by World Bank income classification (Figure 7), we can see that for almost all LICs, this cost is much higher compared to more than half of LMICs, for whom this cost is less than 5%. For Nigeria, which has a lot of extreme poor, the cost is 6%. Another metric to support that LICs need the support of ODA more severely than LMICs do is through the flows of personal remittances and private capital flows. Figure 8 compares net ODA, personal remittances, and foreign direct investment (FDI) net inflows across LICs, LMICs, and UMICs. For LICs, per capita ODA is more than 3x remittances and 2.5x FDI net inflows on average in 2020-24.
